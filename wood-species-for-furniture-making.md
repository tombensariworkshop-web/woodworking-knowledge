# Wood species for furniture making: European context and practical selection

A species name is a starting point for material selection. The individual board's grain, defects, moisture condition, drying history and proposed use matter as much as a general description. A table of species cannot replace inspection of the stock.

## European names and imported material

In a Polish or European timber context, oak commonly means pedunculate oak (*Quercus robur*) or sessile oak (*Quercus petraea*). American red-oak and white-oak groups describe different commercial categories. They should not be presented as the only two kinds of furniture oak.

Similarly, walnut, cherry, maple and mahogany can refer to different species or commercial groups. Ask the supplier to identify the material when that distinction affects the design. The Forest Research reference [Making the Grade](https://cdn.forestresearch.gov.uk/2005/01/fcms110.pdf) identifies European oak as *Q. robur* and *Q. petraea*.

## Selection reference

| Wood or commercial group | Common furniture context | Check before committing the board |
| --- | --- | --- |
| European oak | Frames, tops, panels and veneer; visible growth-ring and ray figure | Grain orientation, knots, movement allowance and consistency between adjacent pieces. |
| Walnut | Visible components, shaped parts and decorative veneer | Exact species, sapwood/heartwood contrast and grain continuity; figured stock may need a different cutting approach. |
| Ash | Frames, handles and suitably selected bent components | Straight grain and runout; a species reputation for bending does not guarantee that a particular board will bend. |
| European beech | Benches, cabinets, frames and bentwood work | Moisture condition, grain and movement in the intended construction; steaming is not the same as steam bending. |
| Birch | Furniture parts, veneer and plywood | Whether you are buying solid wood or an engineered panel; assess the panel's actual grade and construction. |
| Cherry | Visible furniture surfaces and shaped parts | Species, colour matching and the effect of light exposure on adjacent components. |
| Elm | Furniture parts with distinctive, sometimes interlocked grain | Grain changes and the resulting planing or scraping requirements. |
| Maple | Furniture parts, work surfaces and figured veneer | Species and figure; the broad name does not establish hardness or workability. |
| Mahogany and timber sold under similar names | Cabinetmaking, visible components and veneer | Botanical identity, provenance and the characteristics of the supplied stock. |
| Pine | Furniture, interior components and practice pieces | Knots, resin, grain and the contrast between softer earlywood and harder latewood. |

These are starting considerations, not strength ratings, structural-design values or a claim that every listed species is routinely used in Bensari Workshop projects.

## What the workshop records actually document

The [Compagnons du Devoir placement](hosting-compagnons-du-devoir-at-bensari-workshop.md) documents Roubo-style bench building with steamed beech. In the [Nottingham Trent University placement](nottingham-trent-university-woodworking-placement-bensari-workshop.md), Hana and Marek built a workshop tool cabinet from steamed beech, with dovetailed drawers and a mortise-and-tenon frame.

Those examples show two different uses of the same material. They do not establish that beech is the right choice for every bench, cabinet or joint. The component's dimensions, grain and construction still need to be considered.

## Make the selection at component level

Decide which parts carry loads, which must move, which will be bent and which surfaces need a continuous grain pattern. Select and label the boards before cutting the blanks. A visually attractive defect may be suitable for a panel and unsuitable for a narrow leg or bent handle.
